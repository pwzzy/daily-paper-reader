# ABSTRACT 

Optimization by PROmpting (OPRO) directly leverages Large Language Mod-els (LLMs) to generate candidate solutions through natural-language prompt-ing. However, OPRO’s optimization process is difficult to track, which prevents LLMs from capturing useful information accumulated over previous iterations, thereby limiting solution quality. To address this limitation, we propose a Tool-Augmented Strategic Agent (TASA) which summarizes and exploits trajectory to improve solution quality. Specifically, TASA plans the strategy for each step based on the optimization trajectory, which decomposes the optimization process into traceable strategy selections. Moreover, we leverage LLM to construct problem-specific tools and introduce a strategy-guided adaptive tool planner, which effi-ciently utilizes the information of trajectory to update solutions. We establish local convergence under persistent exploration and evaluate the complete frame-work under a shared candidate-evaluation budget. The results support its solution quality and the traceability of strategy-guided search. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) Team et al. (2024); Grattafiori et al. (2024); Guo et al. (2025); Yang et al. (2025) substantially improve their capabilities in reasoning Wei et al. (2022); Gao et al. (2023), planning Yao et al. (2023a); Zhou et al. (2024), and decision-making Yao et al. (2023b), providing possibilities for using LLMs to solve optimization problems. Optimization by PROmpting (OPRO) Yang et al. (2024) leverages an LLM as the optimizer to generate candi-date solutions by iterative prompting process. Specifically, OPRO prompts the LLM to directly generate promising candidate solutions by constructing a prompt that contains a description of the optimization problem and previously evaluated solutions. Due to its advantages, OPRO attracts con-siderable attention, inspiring broader research on LLM-based optimization Guo et al. (2023); Liu et al. (2024b); van Stein & B¨ ack (2025); Zheng et al. (2025); Ye et al. (2025). Unfortunately, the limited traceability of OPRO’s optimization process makes it difficult for LLMs to capture useful information from previous iterations, thereby limiting solution quality. As illus-trated in Figure 1(a), the LLM directly outputs a candidate solution without explicitly stating the intention or trajectory behind its generation. Consequently, OPRO retains only the candidate solu-tions generated across iterations, without an explicit account of how each new solution is generated. This lack of the information hinders LLM from accurately assessing the current search state, thereby limiting ability of OPRO to identify promising search directions. For example, when the candidate set is dominated by similar solutions, the OPRO may struggle to escape the corresponding region of the solution space and discover a better solution. To address this limitation, we propose a Tool-Augmented Strategic Agent (TASA) to plan the strat-egy for each step based on the optimization trajectory. Specifically, TASA constructs an explicit

To maintain adaptability of TASA across tasks, we utilize an LLM to construct a problem-specific tools library based on the characteristics of each optimization problem. TASA constructs tool library through the following two coupled stages. 3Tool Archetype Generation. Given a structured context containing the problem description, con-straints, candidate-solution schema, and strategy definitions, the LLM generates tool archetypes for each of the four strategies. Each archetype describes the intended functionality and behavior rather than providing directly executable code. 

Problem-Specific Tool Archetype Instantiation. Once generated, the tool archetypes are val-idated for structural correctness, safety, and compatibility with the available primitive operators. Valid archetypes are then bound to registered operators and instantiated as callable tools adapted to the current problem, while invalid archetypes are corrected or rejected. The LLM specifies each tool’s strategy, operator sequence, and parameters. Registered operators execute these updates and may perform multiple updates per candidate. Tool-construction prompts appear in the supplementary material. 3.3 OPTIMIZATION STRATEGY PLANNING 

TASA uses an LLM to select optimization strategies from the problem description, available tools, and optimization trajectory. This allows the agent to relate the current search state to the purpose of the next solution update. We let z ∈ Z := {rebuild , repair , polish , escape } denote an optimization strategy, with the four strategies defined as follows. • rebuild reconstructs a solution from a different structural configuration, enabling the ex-ploration of a new region of the search space. • repair focuses on reducing constraint violations and restoring the feasibility of the current solution. • polish performs local refinement around a promising feasible solution to further improve score. • escape applies stronger structural perturbations to move away from a stagnant search re-gion. The allocation among these intentions adapts to the search state. At each replanning iteration k,

sk summarizes solution quality, feasibility, stagnation, remaining budget, and tool feedback from the recorded trajectory. This representation connects the observed search progress to the next op-timization intention. From the results presented in Table 1, it can be observed that TASA achieves the strictly best mean score on five tasks, ties RefSolver for the best result on KP, and ranks second on BGP and PDR. On BGP, it nearly matches RefSolver, with a gaps of only 0.009. Its average z-score across all tasks is 1.806, almost identical to RefSolver’s 1.809. These results show that, under the same evaluation budget, TASA is competitive with specialized solvers while retaining a unified framework across problems with diverse structures and constraints. TASA also consistently outperforms direct LLM-based methods. It exceeds OPRO on every task by 2.376 z-score points on average and surpasses the strongest direct LLM baseline by an average of 1.762 points. The advantage is particularly clear on BGP, VRP, JSSP, and MS, where direct LLM solvers obtain negative mean scores while TASA remains strongly positive. These negative values arise from z-score normalization, as a method receives a negative score when its best feasible score falls below the cross-method mean for the corresponding problem and seed. These results support the combined use of optimization trajectories, strategy planning, and problem-specific tools in TASA under the reported settings. Finally, TASA finds feasible solutions for every seed on all eight tasks and has a standard deviation below 0.2 on six tasks, demonstrating reliable performance across repeated runs. Because some baselines do not reliably produce feasible solutions across random seeds, we use “—” in Table 1 to indicate that no feasible solution was obtained for at least one seed. On four of the five tasks where it ranks first, its standard deviation is no larger than that of RefSolver. Its main weakness is PDR, where it trails RefSolver by 1.145 points, suggesting that more specialized routing and repair tools are needed. On BGP, Solution-Level Feedback plateaus by 20th evaluation, whereas Full Feedback continues to im-prove in subsequent evaluations. On MaxCut, both variants improve until around 40th evaluation and then change little, but Full Feedback consistently maintains higher scores. These results support using the optimization trajectory to provide the LLM strategy planner with information about search progress and stagnation.

In this paper, we propose TASA, which shifts the role of the LLM from directly generating so-lutions to planning optimization strategies based on optimization trajectories and executing them through a strategy-guided adaptive tool planner.
