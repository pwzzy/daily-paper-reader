## ABSTRACT 

Harnesses are increasingly unlocking the potential of large language model (LLM) agents by making their actions more controllable. In automatic heuristic design (AHD) for combinatorial optimization (CO), LLMs iteratively revise solvers using execution and verification feedback. However, these revisions often break previ-ously validated solver behavior, making refinement unstable and costly. We char-acterize this pattern as feasibility oscillation and document it across CO bench-marks, AHD methods, and backbone LLMs. Tracing this oscillation, our analysis reveals that feasibility degradation often follows large solver revisions, while sub-stantial feedback redundancy makes it harder to identify which failures deserve attention. Motivated by these findings, we introduce DiSH, a harness that jointly controls revision direction and scope. It selects representative failure evidence to guide each update and progressively narrows the revision scope as feasible be-havior accumulates. Across nine CO benchmarks, DiSH consistently improves QYI scores and reduces feasibility oscillation, while roughly halving feedback to-ken consumption relative to prior methods that use full feedback. These results establish DiSH as a strong baseline for solver refinement under harness control. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Figure 1: Overall QYI versus token cost. Automatic heuristic design seeks to automate de-velopment for combinatorial optimization algo-rithms. Traditional methods search over heuris-tics predefined by human experts (Burke et al., 2013; Pillay & Qu, 2018), while large language models enable solvers to be generated and revised through iterative feedback (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2026d). This execution-and-feedback loop naturally aligns with the harness, which has become a foundation for building LLM agents (Sumers et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a). Generally, a harness is the system surrounding an LLM that struc-tures its observations and actions, executes its decisions, and returns results. In LLM-based AHD, it therefore determines how execution and verification evidence informs subsequent solver revisions. Despite this central role, the harness-like components of existing LLM-based AHD methods remain largely limited to executing and verifying candidate solvers and returning evaluation feedback to the LLM (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2026d). This is particularly problematic for CO solvers, whose constraints, decisions, and long-horizon logic are tightly coupled. A reported execution error or constraint violation identifies the symptom of a failure, but rarely specifies its root cause in the program or the extent of revision required to correct it. Recent methods regulate the search more finely, but they typically couple revision scope to search operators rather than constraining it independently before generation (Zheng et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026a;b; Wu et al., 2026), while revision priorities largely depend on the model’s interpretation of richer feedback (Yu et al., 2026; Cuong Van Duc et al., 2026; Gungordu et al., 2026; Lv et al., 2026). A revision may repair current failures while disrupting verified behavior, forcing later rounds to recover lost progress. We characterize this repeated loss and recovery as feasibility oscillation , and find it consistently across CO benchmarks, AHD methods, and backbone LLMs, indicating that it is a systemic rather than an isolated issue. Our analysis links feasibility loss to large changes between consecutive solvers and reveals substantial redundancy in the feedback guiding those changes, which obscures repair priorities. These findings motivate joint control of revision scope, which limits how extensively the solver may change, and revision direction, which determines which failures guide the update. Guided by this analysis, we introduce DiSH, a harness framework designed to mitigate feasibility oscillation through joint control of revision direction and scope. As illustrated in Figure 2, DiSH first organizes execution and verification outcomes through hierarchical evaluation, preserving both instance-level failure evidence and the aggregate feasibility state. Direction control distills redundant records into representative repair evidence, making the target of each revision explicit. Scope control progressively restricts the permitted modification as feasible behavior accumulates, reducing the risk that a new revision disrupts verified behavior. Together, these components turn raw evaluation outcomes into revision instructions, guiding iterative revision toward cumulative refinement. Figure 1 summarizes the performance and efficiency comparison. Across nine CO benchmarks, the three DiSH variants achieve higher average QYI while consuming fewer LLM tokens than existing AHD methods. Token-accounting details and the per-backbone search-cost breakdown are provided in Appendix E.3. DiSH also consistently reduces feasibility oscillation and roughly halves feedback-token usage relative to full feedback. Further analysis shows that narrower scope instructions lead to smaller realized revisions, while compact direction evidence preserves repair effectiveness. These results establish DiSH as a strong baseline for harness-controlled solver refinement. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We identify feasibility oscillation as a central obstacle to cumulative solver refinement and system-atically characterize it across CO benchmarks, AHD methods, and backbone LLMs. Our analysis associates feasibility loss with large solver revisions and reveals substantial redundancy in the feed-back used to guide these revisions. • We introduce DiSH, the first AHD harness to our knowledge to demonstrate stable and cumulative improvement across revisions . ## 3 DISH: D IRECTION -AND -S COPE HARNESS FOR CO S OLVER REFINEMENT 

Combinatorial optimization (CO) solvers often involve sequences of interdependent decisions. As discussed in Section 2, revising their code can disrupt feasible behavior instead of improving it. We introduce DiSH, a harness that explicitly controls what to revise and how extensively to change the solver. It aims to stabilize feasibility gains while using feedback tokens efficiently. We first describe the controlled revision loop (Section 3.1). We then present hierarchical evaluation (Section 3.2), adaptive revision scope (Section 3.3), and revision-direction construction (Section 3.4). 3Preprint 3.1 FROM ITERATIVE REVISION TO HARNESS -C ONTROLLED SOLVER REFINEMENT 

We turn evaluation feedback into explicit controls over the direction and scope of solver revisions. 

Why Revisions Need Control. Feasibility oscillation highlights that two issues limit stable re-finement. First, a single LLM call may not resolve failures involving closely linked constraints, decisions, and solver components. Second, repeating revisions can also undo earlier repairs. Thus, 

revision changes the solver, whereas refinement should accumulate feasible behavior across rounds. Given ht, DiSH produces per-instance evaluation records Zt and the feasibility pass rate ρt. It then constructs a revision direction dt, a scope instruction ηt, and a prompt pt for the next LLM revision: Evaluation: (Zt, ρ t) = E(ht, Dtrain ),

Construction: dt = Gdir (Z0: t), ηt = Gstep (ρt),

Generation: pt = H(Zt, d t, η t), ht+1 ∼ M LLM (· | T , h t, p t).

(4) Here, E evaluates instances in successive stages. Gdir selects and organizes revision targets from current and historical evaluation evidence, while Gstep determines the permitted scope from ρt. H

combines the evaluation records and these controls into revision instructions pt, which is then used to generate the next solver ht+1 . Appendix C provides the complete prompt structure and examples. Rather than exposing all observed failure records directly to the LLM, direction control priori-tizes them into representative repair evidence while retaining previously verified behavior. Scope control permits less extensive changes at higher pass rates, encouraging smaller revision distances 

∆( ht+1 , h t). Together, these controls aim to reduce regressions and support stable feasibility gains within the finite revision budget. Algorithm 1 in Appendix B gives the complete refinement loop. 3.2 HIERARCHICAL FEASIBILITY EVALUATION AND FEEDBACK 

We evaluate each instance in three stages to identify where it fails and provide feedback for revision. 

From Outcome Evaluation to Failure Localization. Existing AHD pipelines mainly use execu-tion and verification outcomes to score solver candidates (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2024) or provide feedback (Chen et al., 2026d), without explicitly localizing fail-ures for revision control. However, execution errors, invalid outputs, and constraint violations arise from various solving stages and require different repairs. DiSH therefore constructs a hierarchical evaluation pipeline that records both the failure stage and the associated diagnostic information. 

Stage-Wise Instance Evaluation. To make evaluation feedback more diagnostic, DiSH applies three checks in order and records the first failed stage rather than reducing its outcome to a single score. For each instance x ∈ D train , Stage I checks execution within time and memory limits. Stage II checks output validity, and Stage III checks satisfaction of problem constraints. For exam-ple, a routing solver may time out on one instance but return a correctly formatted route that serves a customer twice on another. These failures call for different repairs: improving execution efficiency and correcting solution construction, respectively. Appendix B.2.1 provides the detailed criteria. 

Hierarchical Evaluation Records. The resulting record is zt(x) = ( st(x), e t(x), q t(x)) , where 

st(x) denotes the terminal evaluation stage, et(x) contains the corresponding error information, and 

qt(x) is the objective value of a feasible solution. Depending on the terminal stage, et(x) may con-tain an exception, a parsing error, or a constraint violation. Failure records have no objective value, while successful records have no error information. The records from all instances are collected as 

Zt = {zt(x)}x∈D train . The hierarchical records support revision control at two levels. Aggregated across instances, they yield the feasibility pass rate ρt defined in Eq. (2), which informs the per-mitted revision scope. At the instance level, failure stages and diagnostics provide repair evidence, while successful records identify behavior to preserve. 3.3 FEASIBILITY -A DAPTIVE REVISION SCOPE 

We adapt the permitted revision scope to the current pass rate, allowing less extensive changes as more instances become feasible. 

Conditioning Scope on Feasibility. Failure evidence indicates what requires repair, but not how broadly the solver should be revised. At low ρt, fundamental failures may require substantial revi-4Preprint sions. At high ρt, such revisions risk disrupting verified behavior, as shown in Figure 4. We therefore use ηt = Gstep (ρt) to progressively narrow the permitted revision scope as feasibility increases. 

Semantic Revision Levels and Modes. To encourage smaller revision distances as feasibility im-proves, DiSH uses a prompt instruction ηt to specify the permitted semantic scope. We distinguish three revision levels. Parameter revisions adjust numerical constants without changing solver logic. 

Strategy revisions modify decision rules or local procedures. Algorithm revisions replace the core solving approach. The scope rule ηt = Gstep (ρt) selects among four modes with progressively tighter restrictions. B ROAD permits all three revision levels. M ODERATE favors strategy revisions and permits algorithm replacement only when failures indicate a structural mismatch. CONSER -

VATIVE allows parameter changes and narrowly targeted strategy revisions but prohibits algorithm replacement. At full feasibility, P ROTECTED instructs the LLM to preserve the solver by default, permitting only beneficial parameter changes that do not compromise currently feasible instances. Distilling Evaluation Feedback into Revision Direc-tion. Revision scope limits how extensively the solver may change, but a successful revision also depends on how the LLM uses evaluation feedback to decide what to change. The main obstacle is redundancy: many instance-level records describe the same failure, consuming tokens and making distinct repair needs harder to identify. Fig-ure 6 illustrates this redundancy in a refinement example. Previously feasible instances I1 and I2 now fail with the same IndexError as I3. The number of failure records increases from three to five, while the number of distinct failure groups remains three. To further quantify feedback redundancy, we analyze 69,890 failure records collected across revision rounds from five methods on nine benchmarks. Of these records, 95.6% share a failure pattern with another instance in the same evaluation. In 88.7% of solver evalu-ations, at least two instances exhibit the same failure pattern. These results motivate grouping related failures and distilling representative evidence instead of passing all records to the LLM.

Each group Gs,a t contains instances with the same failure stage s and error category a, and Gt collects all nonempty failure groups. Alternative Direction Constructions. The direction constructor is chosen from three alternatives: Gdir ∈ {G GWRS , GCRS , GGRS }.Each independently constructs dt from representative failures and successful records. As shown in Figure 7, they use 42.3–51.8% of the tokens required by full feedback while preserving the measured rate at which failure modes are repaired in the next revision. Thus, selecting one direction strategy is sufficient to substantially reduce feedback without diminishing the measured repair effectiveness. • Group-Wise Representative Sampling (GWRS) selects one representative from every current fail-ure group. For each G ∈ G t, it chooses the largest instance, rG = arg max x∈G n(x), where n(x)

is the input size, with ties broken by instance identifier. This rule preserves group coverage with-out repeating similar errors. The largest instance is chosen to expose the failure on a structurally richer input. Groups are reconstructed after each evaluation: repaired instances provide examples of behavior to preserve, while unresolved failures remain available for later revisions. • Correction Representative Sampling (CRS) uses consecutive evaluations to relate current failures to the preceding revision. It selects the smallest instance rG = arg min x∈G n(x) from each failure group, reducing incidental input complexity and making the remaining error easier to isolate. CRS additionally identifies the regression set Ut = Pt−1 ∩ F t, containing instances that were previously feasible but now fail. This comparison also annotates current failures as regressed, persistent, or changed, allowing dt to emphasize behavior disrupted or left unresolved by the previous revision. • Scope-Aware Group Representative Sampling (GRS) concentrates direction evidence on high-priority failure groups. It identifies a focal stage from the stage-level failure distribution and ranks the groups by stage priority and coverage ct(G) := |G|/|F t|. It then retains the top k(ρt) groups, where fewer groups are retained as feasibility increases, and selects the largest instance from each retained group. Consequently, low-feasibility states expose a broader range of repair needs, whereas high-feasibility states focus the revision on fewer, higher-priority targets.

Figure 12 presents three representative revisions from DiSH runs, illustrating how different scope modes guide solver modification. On Pedigree, only one of five demonstration instances passes, so BROAD mode permits aggressive changes to the core algorithm. The revision replaces the variable elimination and factor graph hybrid with Mendelian backtracking and pruning, raising feasibility from 1/5 to 5/5. On Protein Design, M ODERATE mode applies at 2/5 feasibility. The revision introduces chain-aware residue repair and centroid coordination, raising demonstration feasibility from 2/5 to 5/5. On CVRP, four of five instances already pass, and the C ONSERVATIVE revision makes targeted parameter adjustments. It reduces the neighbor count, local search iterations, and relocation threshold without changing the routing or search logic, raising feasibility from 4/5 to 

5/5. The purple regions mark P ROTECTED mode, entered after full feasibility is reached. This mode preserves the solver by default and permits only parameter changes with a concrete benefit and no adverse effect on currently feasible instances. All three runs maintain full feasibility throughout the remaining iterations. Together, these cases illustrate how DiSH adapts revision scope to feasibility, allowing substantial repairs when needed and prioritizing preservation as feasibility improves. 

## 5 CONCLUSION 

This work shows that stable LLM-based refinement of combinatorial optimization solvers depends not only on the feedback itself, but also on how that feedback is translated into solver revisions. Existing approaches largely leave two key decisions to the LLM: what to change and how much to change . This can cause revisions to disrupt previously feasible behavior and lead to feasibil-ity oscillation. DiSH makes these decisions explicit by controlling revision direction and scope: representative failure groups identify what should be addressed, while the current feasibility state determines how extensively the solver should be revised. Across nine combinatorial optimization problems and four backbone LLMs, DiSH achieves the highest overall average QYI, produces more 9Preprint stable refinement trajectories, and uses roughly half the feedback tokens required by full feedback. These results suggest that controlling the revision process itself provides another effective way to improve automatic heuristic design, complementing advances in search and solver generation.

## A RELATED WORK 

A.1 HARNESS FOR LANGUAGE -M ODEL AGENTS 

Before the agent harness emerged as an explicit systems concept, external environments already supported agent interaction and feedback. TextWorld (Côté et al., 2019), ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021), and WebShop (Yao et al., 2022) provide executable environments for text, embodied, and web tasks. ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) integrates reasoning, action, and observation, while Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023) carries failure feedback across trials through verbal reflection. Research has expanded to execution, verification, and interface design. SWE-bench (Jimenez et al., 2024) tests repository edits, while WebArena (Zhou et al., 2024) checks functional correctness against environment states. SWE-agent (Yang et al., 2024) shows that interface design can improve performance without chang-ing the model. Together, these developments mark a transition from interaction environments to unified harnesses integrating execution, tooling, context, state maintenance, and verification. Recent work further uses execution histories and failures to organize subsequent runs. Life-Harness (Xu et al., 2026) distills reusable interventions from recurring failures in training inter-action histories while keeping the harness fixed during evaluation. AgentTether (Zhao et al., 2026b) localizes critical subtrajectories within failed executions and provides targeted guidance and runtime interventions for re-execution of the same agent. These works illustrate a shift from using execution outcomes for evaluation toward using them to organize subsequent execution. A.2 LLM S FOR OPTIMIZATION MODELING AND SOLVING 

LLMs are increasingly used to automate optimization modeling by translating natural-language problem descriptions into mathematical formulations and solver-executable code. Training-based approaches develop models specialized for this task through synthesized supervision. ORLM com-bines semi-automated data generation with open-source model training (Huang et al., 2025), while OptMATH constructs scalable bidirectional modeling data with forward verification and rejection sampling (Lu et al., 2025).SIRL further grounds training in solver-verifiable signals over generated models and executable code, providing rewards for syntactic validity, feasibility, and solution qual-ity (Chen et al., 2025). Opt-Miner combines tree-guided data synthesis with reinforcement learning that rewards retrieval quality and modeling accuracy, training agents to acquire external knowledge for optimization problems with knowledge gaps (Liu et al., 2026c). Complementary prompt-based and agentic approaches structure the modeling process itself. Opti-MUS formulates models, generates solver code, and uses execution feedback to diagnose and repair modeling or implementation errors (Ahmaditeshnizi et al., 2024). OptiTree organizes formulation through a hierarchical modeling tree (Liu et al., 2025b), while MM-Agent studies broader mathe-matical modeling through staged workflows and hierarchical knowledge (Liu et al., 2025a). LEAN-LLM-OPT dynamically constructs agentic workflows for large-scale auto-formulation and delegates structured data operations to auxiliary tools (Liang et al., 2026). MIRROR combines hierarchical exemplar retrieval with execution-driven revision of mathematical models and solver code (Shi et al., 2026). Evaluation has also advanced through structural-equivalence measures (Wang et al., 2025b) and industrial-scale benchmarks derived from real mixed-integer programs (Li et al., 2026). Beyond formulation, LLMs have been trained as end-to-end CO solvers (Jiang et al., 2025) and used to automate solver-level decisions, including instance-specific MIP configuration (Luo et al., 2026) and branching-policy discovery (Hou et al., 2026). These studies focus on problem formulation, direct solution generation, or solver-level decisions. A related line of work instead uses LLMs to automatically design and refine executable heuristics. A.3 LLM-BASED AUTOMATIC HEURISTIC DESIGN 

Heuristic design for combinatorial optimization has long relied on expert knowledge. Hyper-heuristics and algorithm configuration methods search within predefined spaces of heuristics, com-ponents, or parameters (Burke et al., 2013; Pillay & Qu, 2018; Burke et al., 2019; Hutter et al., 2009; López-Ibáñez et al., 2016). LLMs extend this search to executable programs (Liu et al., 2026b). Fun-Search (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024) evolves functions within fixed program skeletons, EoH (Liu et al., 2024) evolves heuristic ideas and implementations, and ReEvo (Ye et al., 2024) incorporates 16 Preprint reflection. Together, these methods establish iterative revision through repeated program evaluation and verification. Subsequent research focuses on organizing and regulating this iterative search process. Approaches promote candidate diversity and branching (Dat et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025), introduce different modification granularities (Chen et al., 2026a;c), and adapt search behavior to current states (Chen et al., 2026b; Wu et al., 2026; Lai et al., 2026). Complementary extensions explore complete metaheuristics and coupled operators (van Stein & Bäck, 2025; Zhao et al., 2026a), collaborative search (Hu & Zhang, 2025), multiple objectives (Yao et al., 2025), and complementary heuristic sets (Liu et al., 2026a). Others improve guidance and evaluation efficiency (Wu et al., 2025) or adapt the generating model (Huang et al., 2026). These developments broaden both the algorithmic structures explored and the mechanisms governing their evolution. Alongside these advances in search design, other work focuses on enriching and organizing the evidence used to guide revisions. HeuriGym (Chen et al., 2026d), LaGO (Yu et al., 2026), and ReVEL (Cuong Van Duc et al., 2026) provide execution, analytical, and structured feedback, re-spectively. PathWise (Gungordu et al., 2026) reuses search histories, AHD Agent (Lv et al., 2026) acquires targeted evidence, and ARES (Yang et al., 2026) combines structural ablation with accumu-lated design experience. These approaches extend revision guidance beyond aggregate performance toward richer, more structured, and more targeted evidence. A.4 HARNESSES IN LLM-BASED SOLVER REFINEMENT 

For solver refinement, harness design must address not only evaluation and feedback, but also how each revision is controlled. HeuriGym (Chen et al., 2026d) integrates execution, output parsing, and verification to provide instance-level feedback. CO-Bench (Sun et al., 2026) and ALE-Bench (Ima-juku et al., 2025) evaluate complete solvers through execution and performance measurement. How-ever, mapping evaluation states to explicit revision controls remains underexplored. This gap concerns both revision scope and revision direction . Scope determines how extensively a solver may change. Existing methods typically couple it to search operators rather than constrain it independently before generation (Zheng et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026a;b; Wu et al., 2026). Direc-tion specifies where changes should focus, yet priorities largely depend on the model’s interpretation of richer feedback (Yu et al., 2026; Cuong Van Duc et al., 2026; Gungordu et al., 2026; Lv et al., 2026). The practical challenge is to repair current failures without disrupting previously verified behavior. This motivates exploring how a harness can use the execution and verification evidence it collects to jointly control revision direction and scope.

Rather than exposing all instance-level failures to the LLM, DiSH groups failures with shared signatures and selects representative evidence from these groups, while retaining successful cases as behavioral anchors to discourage regression. Based on this common representation, we consider three direction strategies, GWRS, CRS, and GRS, which differ in how failure evidence is prioritized, selected, and organized. The resulting guidance is dy-namically constructed from the evaluation records available at each refinement round.

## D METRIC DEFINITIONS 

We employ three groups of metrics to evaluate the magnitude of solver revisions, the performance of the resulting solutions, and the dynamics of the refinement process. D.1 REVISION DISTANCE 

Section 2 uses revision distance to quantify the magnitude of each solver revision. For a solver program h, we tokenize its Python source code and remove comments and formatting-related tokens, including indentation, line breaks, and end-of-file markers. Let (z1, . . . , z m) denote the resulting token sequence. We define T (h) = {(zi, z i+1 , . . . , z i+4 ) | 1 ≤ i ≤ m − 4} as the set of all contiguous five-token subsequences. 26 Preprint 

Jaccard Similarity. For two consecutive solvers ht and ht+1 , we measure their code similarity using the Jaccard index: 

J(ht, h t+1 ) = |T (ht) ∩ T (ht+1 )||T (ht) ∪ T (ht+1 )| .

Revision Distance. The revision distance is defined as the complement of their Jaccard similarity: 

∆( ht+1 , h t) = 1 − J(ht, h t+1 ).

A smaller ∆( ht+1 , h t) indicates greater code similarity between consecutive solvers, whereas a larger value indicates a more extensive revision. We do not normalize variable names, canonical-ize abstract syntax trees, or otherwise account for semantic equivalence between programs. Thus, 

∆( ht+1 , h t) serves as a transparent proxy for code-level revision magnitude rather than semantic distance. D.2 SOLUTION PERFORMANCE METRICS

Compared Methods. All methods operate in the same complete-solver refinement setting. For the five AHD baselines, adaptations are limited to incorporating the shared initialization, complete-solver evalua-tion and output adaptation, minimal compatibility adjustments, and token accounting and logging. Their native search operators and method-specific search procedures remain unchanged. All AHD baselines use the same demonstration-set scalar fitness, without method-specific fitness tuning or held-out feedback. 

Shared Initialization. For each problem, we generate three independent initial complete solvers. Within each initialization, all methods start from the same solver, denoted by θ0, so that differences in starting conditions do not confound the comparison. Each method is evaluated with three inde-pendent repeats per initialization under each backbone, yielding nine runs for every combination of problem and backbone. For the population-based AHD baselines, initialization is adapted to in-corporate θ0 while preserving the subsequent search procedure. EoH, HiFo-Prompt, and RefineEvo initialize generation zero with the singleton population {θ0}. ReEvo and HSEvo first evaluate θ0 and then generate seed-conditioned descendants, retaining θ0 as an elite or best reference. ## H LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

Limitations. The main limitation of DiSH is that its control mechanism is designed primarily around feasibility, with less explicit control over solution quality. As the feasibility pass rate in-33 Preprint creases, DiSH progressively narrows the revision scope to preserve verified behavior and reduce regressions. Once full feasibility is reached, revisions therefore become increasingly conservative. This improves refinement stability, but may also limit opportunities for further quality improvement. In addition, the current scope thresholds and direction strategies are manually specified. These rules are simple and interpretable, but may not adapt equally well to different problems, backbone LLMs, or refinement stages. Overall, DiSH provides effective control for stable feasibility, while quality-aware and more adaptive control remain open directions. 

Future Work. In the future, we can extend DiSH toward more flexible control of both feasibility and solution quality. One direction is to continue improving solution quality after feasibility has been established, while explicitly protecting verified feasible behavior. Another direction is to learn the direction and scope policies from refinement trajectories. This could replace fixed thresholds and manually designed rules with controls that adapt to the current solver state, problem structure, and backbone LLM. A further extension is to combine such control with population-based automatic heuristic design, where multiple solver candidates are refined in parallel and can provide comple-mentary search information. Together, these directions could make revision control more adaptive while supporting continued improvement beyond feasibility. 34
