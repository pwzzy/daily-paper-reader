# HEMO: Harness Self-Evolution for Multi-Objective Combinatorial Optimization 

Jianghan Zhu, Cong Zhang, Ni Zhang, Yifan Yang, Yew Soon Ong, and Zhiguang Cao 

Abstract —Automatic operator design (AOD) has increasingly adopted large language models (LLMs) to generate problem-specific search and variation operators for multi-objective com-binatorial optimization (MOCO). Yet most methods evolve the operators while leaving the process that discovers them fixed. This limits adaptation as new Pareto trade-offs and operator interactions change where and how the search should proceed. We introduce HEMO, which makes the AOD harness itself a self-evolving optimization target. HEMO maintains a persistent harness state and updates one module at a time, revising the logic that determines which experience to retrieve, which operators to improve, which parents to select, and how to formulate the orchestration of search steps. Downstream solution quality and runtime guide these updates, while a discounted UCB scheduler directs adaptation toward promising modules as their utility changes. A persistent memory filesystem preserves candidate outcomes and diagnostic feedback across harness states, enabling successive states to build on accumulated optimization experience. Experiments across eight MOCO tasks and two structurally distinct evolutionary backbones demonstrate strong performance against conventional evolutionary, neural, and LLM-based AOD baselines, together with effective transfer to related tasks. HEMO extends automatic operator design beyond evolving individual operators to improving the search process that discovers them. 

Index Terms —Evolutionary computing and genetic algorithms, heuristics design, heuristic methods, language models, multi-objective combinatorial optimization, optimization.

Despite their different search mechanisms, performance often relies on problem-specific representations, and variation operators, making the solving of a new MOCO problem closely tied to the design of an effective search procedure. Within MOCO, a particularly relevant direction is the automatic design of problem-specific search and variation operators, which we refer to as automatic operator design (AOD). LLMs have been used to generate evolutionary operators with different trade-offs [16], [17], while newer frameworks further emphasize population diversity and operator interactions [18], [19]. A fixed AOD harness cannot revise its predefined control logic, while independently generated high-level procedures lack a persistent control-state trajectory. An AOD run may produce hundreds or thousands of evaluated candidates with their performance, lineage, and search context, yet storing this history alone does not ensure useful evidence remains accessible. Motivated by these observations, we introduce HEMO ,a stateful AOD harness for LLM-based operator design in multi-objective combinatorial optimization. HEMO maintains a persistent harness state and progressively adapts its search control through localized state transitions. • We introduce a stateful harness self-evolution perspective for LLM driven AOD. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Automated Algorithm Design 

Automated algorithm design (AAD) aims to reduce the expert effort required to construct effective algorithms by automating design processes such as algorithm selection, configuration, component design, and code execution and validation [25]– [27]. Automated heuristic design (AHD) forms an important research branch of this broader field, with methods ranging 3

from hyper-heuristics [12] and genetic programming [26] to the automatic generation and composition of search operators [28]. Although these approaches automate different parts of algorithm development, the underlying heuristic spaces and composition rules are commonly specified by human experts. LLM reasoning and code generation have substantially expanded the scope of automated design. FunSearch evolves executable programs through island-based selection and gen-eration [13], while EoH, ReEvo, and MCTS-AHD combine LLM generation with evolutionary, reflective, or tree-based search to improve one single-objective optimization heuristic component [14], [15], [29]. SimpleEvol further organizes LLM-driven heuristic generation within a lightweight agent loop designed to minimize human-specified priors [30]. Beyond individual combinatorial heuristics, LLaMEA and LLaMEA-BO evolve complete algorithms for continuous black-box and Bayesian optimization, respectively [31], [32]. Outside opti-mization, AlphaEvolve demonstrates evolutionary code search over broader scientific and computational algorithms [22]. More recent work has begun to move the design target beyond solely heuristics. MoH searches over heuristic optimizers that generate task-specific heuristics [23], while related work explores higher-level algorithm structures and control policies [33]. These developments indicate a broader shift from designing isolated heuristics toward changing increasingly large portions of the algorithm design process itself. Yet the execution structure governing LLM access to accumulated experience, forms design context, invokes evaluation, and maintains search state is still typically specified by human designers. Designing a framework that enables the stateful self-evolution of this surrounding control structure is therefore the focus of our work. 

B. Multi-Objective Optimization 

Most of recent research can be divided into two pathways. The first improves multi-objective solvers through manually designed evolutionary mechanisms or learned solution policies and search components. Classical multi-objective evolution-ary algorithms (MOEAs) include dominance-based methods such as NSGA-II [7], decomposition-based methods like MOEA/D [8], and archive- or local-search-based approaches such as Pareto Local Search (PLS) [34], two-phase Pareto Local Search (2PPLS) [3], and Simple Evolutionary Multi-objective Optimization (SEMO) [35]. Their effectiveness depends strongly on carefully designed mechanisms for se-lection, population management, neighborhood exploration, and diversity preservation, motivating numerous modified and hybrid variants [36], [37]. Neural approaches instead learn solution policies or search components from data. PMOCO employs a single preference-conditioned model to generate solutions under different objective preferences [9]. NHDE explicitly promotes Pareto-set diversity [10], while EMNH improves learning efficiency through meta-learning [38].Neuro-PLS embeds learned components into Pareto local search [39], while other work improves preference conditioning and cross-size generalization through learned weight representations [40]. Although neural methods reduce repeated handcrafting and enable fast GPU-based inference, they generally require model training and retain a largely predefined optimization backbone. The second pathway uses LLMs to design problem-specific heuristics and variation operators within prescribed multi-objective solvers, a setting we refer to as automatic operator design (AOD). Here, an operator is an algorithmic component that constructs, modifies, selects, or otherwise guides candidate solutions, including heuristics studied in AHD when they serve such a role. Huang et al. [16] use LLMs to iteratively refine evolutionary operators. MEoH treats heuristic design as a multi-objective optimization problem and searches for non-dominated heuristics [17], while REMoH integrates LLM-generated oper-ators into NSGA-II with clustering-based reflection to promote diversity [41]. MPaGE combines LLM-based heuristic gen-eration with Pareto-front-grid-guided population management under SEMO [18]. E2OC further models dependencies among multiple search operators and uses Monte Carlo tree search to explore their design combinations [19]. These methods extend LLM-based AAD toward increasingly complex multi-objective solver components. However, they primarily evolve operators 

within a prescribed search process, leaving its higher-level harness unchanged. 

C. Harness Engineering and Evolution 

The performance of an LLM-based system depends not only on its backbone model, but also on the surrounding execution machinery that determines what information the model receives, which tools it can invoke, how intermediate state is recorded and updated, and how multi-step execution is guided by feedback. Mechanisms now associated with this layer were initially studied separately, including retrieval-augmented generation [42], interleaved reasoning and acting [43], and explicit self-reflection [44]. Recent work increasingly integrates these mechanisms into a broader agent harness . Natural-Language Agent Harnesses separate control, contracts, and persistent state from the underlying runtime [45], while recent surveys characterize harness engineering as spanning execution environments, tool interfaces, context and memory management, lifecycle and orchestration, execution monitoring, and output validation [21]. A closely related line of work also automates broader portions of agent and optimization systems [46], [47]. ADAS searches over code-defined agent systems [48], AFlow formulates agentic workflow generation as search over executable workflow graphs [49], and the Darwin Gödel Machine allows coding agents to iteratively modify their own implementations [50]. In optimization, AutoEP uses an external LLM-based controller to adapt the hyperparameters of a structurally fixed metaheuristic [51], while OKAEM adapts learned evolutionary operators using prior and online optimization knowledge [52]. CORAL employs autonomous single- and multi-agent evolution to determine what experience to retrieve, when to evaluate candidates, and what knowledge to preserve [24]. Although these approaches automate context construction, workflows, and search control, they do not generally treat the complete task-specific execution harness as the direct search object. More recent studies explicitly optimize the harness surround-ing an LLM. Meta-Harness performs outer-loop search over harness code, allowing an agentic proposer to inspect previous 4

source code, evaluation scores, and execution traces through a filesystem before constructing new harness candidates [20]. Self-Harness similarly identifies recurring weaknesses from execution traces, proposes targeted modifications, and validates the resulting harness [53]. Existing harness evolution studies primarily target general agent execution in coding, reasoning, and interactive tasks. HEMO instead studies harness evolution for LLM-based automatic operator design in MOCO. It main-tains a persistent AOD harness whose decision modules adapt through localized state transitions while the execution-critical scaffold remains fixed, connecting harness adaptation directly to downstream multi-objective optimization performance and long-term operator design experience. III. P RELIMINARIES 

A. Multi-Objective Combinatorial Optimization 

Problem Formulation and Pareto Optimality. A multi-objective combinatorial optimization problem (MOCOP) seeks to optimize multiple, potentially conflicting objectives over a discrete feasible space [54]. Let X be the feasible solution set and M the number of objectives. Without loss of generality, we consider the minimization form

fm(x) ≤ fm(y), ∀m ∈ { 1, . . . , M }, (2) and the inequality is strict for at least one objective. A feasible solution x∗ is Pareto optimal if there exists no other feasible solution that dominates it. The collection of all Pareto-optimal solutions constitutes the Pareto set (PS), and its image in the objective space constitutes the Pareto front (PF). For many MOCOPs, computing the complete Pareto set is computationally prohibitive, and practical solvers therefore seek a finite approximation of the PF under limited computational budgets [5], [55]. A useful approximation should simultane-ously approach the true PF and maintain sufficient coverage across different trade-offs among objectives [55]. 

Multi-Objective Evolutionary Algorithms. Multi-objective evolutionary algorithms (MOEAs) construct such approxima-tions by evolving a population or archive of candidate solu-tions. Early Pareto-based evolutionary approaches established selection at the population level based on dominance and diversity, with later methods developing alternative mechanisms to balance convergence and diversity. The MOEA behavior thus depends on both problem-specific variation operators and higher-level mechanisms to select, retain, and propagate solutions across iterations. We use two structurally distinct back-bones, namely SEMO [35], with incremental archive updates, and NSGA-II [7], which uses generational population evolution with non-dominated sorting and diversity preservation. 

B. AOD Harnesses 

Agent Harnesses. An LLM-based system includes an execution layer that governs the context presented to the model, inter-actions with tools and evaluators, persistent state, and control across multiple model calls. We refer to this execution layer as an agent harness . In automatic operator design, the harness governs the outer process through which an LLM searches over operator candidates. It determines how prior search experience is exposed, how design context is assembled, how candidate generation and evaluation are coordinated, and how search state is maintained across iterations. We use the term AOD harness 

for this task-specific execution structure. The methodology in Section IV focuses on how such a harness can maintain a persistent state and adapt its search-control behavior over time. IV. M ETHODOLOGY 

HEMO is an AOD harness with self-evolving decision mod-ules for multi-objective combinatorial optimization (MOCO). Its state evolves through local module updates, while a memory filesystem retains experience across states. These updates adapt experience retrieval, target and parent selection, and candidate evolution, with new optimization experience continuously recorded in memory. Figure 2 summarizes the overall workflow. HEMO separates a fixed execution scaffold from the set of decision modules, with harness state transitions guided by the module-specific reward computed from downstream task performance. Three LLM agents play distinct roles in this process. The Proposer Agent specifies an updated version of one selected module in a JSON blueprint using the current harness state, task information, and recent harness traces. The Realizer Agent 

converts this blueprint into executable Python code grounded in the current harness implementation. The resulting child harness is then operationalized by the Executor Agent within a bounded harness evaluation episode, interacting with the memory filesystem and the task evaluator, and producing evaluated candidates and search records. Memory persists across episodes, accumulating experience along the outer trajectory. 

A. Stateful Harness Self-Evolution 

Harness initialization. HEMO begins from a manually specified seed harness that defines the basic execution interface and a simple reference implementation for each evolvable module. To avoid committing the search to a single manually designed starting point, a small set of alternative harness blueprints, each specifying all evolvable modules, is generated and realized to form an initial harness pool. These states are evaluated to construct an initial Pareto archive over harness states, from which the initial current state H0 is selected for subsequent harness self-evolution. 

Harness state transitions. We represent the current harness state as Ht = ( Ct, B t), where Ct denotes the full harness code and Bt denotes its corresponding blueprint that records the current specifications of all evolvable modules. The memory filesystem at step t is denoted by Mt and serves as an external mechanism for retaining optimization experience across harness states. At each outer step after initialization, one evolvable module mt ∈ M is selected for modification, where M = {m(1) , . . . , m (K)} denotes the set of evolvable modules. B. Persistent Memory and Long-Horizon Feedback 

Long-horizon AOD can produce hundreds or thousands of evaluated candidates, making it impractical to expose the complete search history to an LLM at every step. Moreover, retaining all records in external storage does not imply that they remain effectively available to the model. Finite context windows and repeated summarization can bias the working context toward recent attempts. HEMO therefore separates persistent storage from effective retrieval through an external memory filesystem and explicit long-horizon feedback mechanisms. 

Structured experience memory. The memory filesystem stores the search experience of the evaluated operator candidates outside the transient LLM context as structured optimization events. Each record can retain candidate designs, evaluation re-sults, parent information, validity or failure information, Pareto-related status, operator context, and other search metadata. This provides a persistent empirical history that remains reusable across successive harness states. 

Periodic long-term feedback. HEMO periodically transforms accumulated search history into compact diagnostic feedback. These tools summarize the evolving search trajectory and identify prolonged stagnation or repeated search patterns, allowing subsequent harness modules to access abstracted signals without rescanning the complete history. We refer to each periodic invocation of these tools as a heartbeat , consisting primarily of a summary report and a stagnation report, and the latest report of each type is maintained as the only active reference record. 

C. MOCO-Specific Harness Design 

HEMO separates its AOD harness into a fixed execution scaffold and a set of evolvable decision roles. As shown in Fig. 2, the fixed scaffold preserves the AOD harness lifecycle, while the evolvable roles capture decisions whose preferred behavior depends on the current search state. 

Fixed execution scaffold. Four operational interfaces remain fixed throughout the entire stateful harness self-evolution process. 1) Evaluation interface. It specifies solver configu-ration, dataset generation, evaluation instances, and feedback construction independently of the harness loop, preventing harness changes from altering the evaluation protocol or gaming the intended objective. 2) Multi-objective archive interface. It merges newly generated operator candidates into the candidate Pareto archive using a fixed non-dominated sorting rule, keeping Pareto filtering and diversity preservation consistent throughout harness state transition. 3) Diagnostic and heartbeat inter-face. It exposes standardized tools for summarizing the search history and diagnosing stagnation patterns. The heartbeat tools and callable interfaces remain fixed, while the decision modules determine how the resulting diagnostic feedback is retrieved and utilized. 4) Memory-writing interface. It records evaluated candidates and search events using a common schema, ensuring a consistent empirical history across successive harness states. This separates persistent bookkeeping from the adaptive policy that determines which experience becomes active context. Examples of evolved harness states and generated operators are in Appendix F and J. 

Evolvable decision roles. Within this scaffold, HEMO exposes four high-level roles whose behavior can be modified by the harness state transition.  

> Experience Retrieval.

This role determines which historical candidate records and reports of the previously discovered search patterns should be surfaced from memory filesystem for the current AOD step. Its purpose is not merely to compress context, but to decide adaptively which parts of the long-horizon optimization history are currently useful.  

> Target Selection.

When the underlying optimization solver exposes multiple search components or evolutionary operators, this role determines which component should receive the next design intervention. Such a decision is particularly relevant to multi-component evolutionary solvers such as NSGA-II [7], where the marginal value of improving one operator depends on the current behavior of the surrounding operators. For solvers with only one evolvable component, this role can degenerate to a trivial choice and need not introduce additional control complexity.

> Candidate Evolution.

This role determines the search strategy used to produce successive candidates within a bounded harness evaluation episode. It controls how the search pro-gresses from one evaluated candidate to the next, coordinating candidate generation, evaluation, and subsequent iterations. E XPERIMENTS 

Benchmarks and generalization protocol. We evaluate HEMO on eight MOCO tasks spanning four representative problem families [36], covering knapsack, vehicle routing, TSP, and scheduling. Here, TSP, KP, MDKP, CVRP, SPDP, and FJSP denote the traveling salesman problem, knapsack problem, multi-dimensional knapsack problem, capacitated vehicle routing problem, selective pickup and delivery problem, and flexible job shop scheduling problem, respectively. The prefixes Bi- and Tri- denote bi-objective and tri-objective formulations, respectively. The source-to-target pairs are Bi-TSP → Tri-TSP, Bi-KP → Bi-MDKP, Bi-CVRP → Bi-SPDP, and Bi-FJSP → Tri-FJSP, where the source task is used for in-distribution harness self-evolution and the target task for intra-family cross-task generalization with the fixed harness state. Synthetic problems are evaluated at multiple sizes, while FJSP uses the Brandimarte benchmark [58] instances for final evaluation. Detailed task formulations are provided in Appendix B. 

HEMO configuration. We instantiate HEMO with both SEMO [35] and NSGA-II [7] as two structurally distinct optimization backbones. Harness self-evolution allows at most 200 harness state evaluations for both SEMO and NSGA-II. Each SEMO harness evaluation generates 50 candidates with population size 5, while each NSGA-II evaluation allows 100 complete operator combination evaluations with operator and combination population sizes of 10. 

Objectives and evaluation metrics. During AOD, all generated operator candidates are evaluated jointly by solution quality and computational efficiency, represented by negative Hypervolume (HV) and runtime under a common lower-is-better convention. Compared baselines and evaluation protocol. We compare HEMO with three groups of baselines: (1) Conventional MOEAs , including SEMO, NSGA-II, MOEA/D [8], and PFG-MOEA [37], (2) Neural MOCO methods , represented by PMOCO [9] and (3) LLM-based AOD methods , including EoH [14], MEoH [17], MPaGE [18], and E2OC [19]. The HEMO Proposer, Realizer, and Executor agents use Claude Opus 4.8, GPT-5, and GPT-4.1-nano, respectively. During inference, all LLM-based AOD methods use GPT-5-nano unless otherwise stated, with identical candidate generation budgets of 2,000 for SEMO and 4,000 for NSGA-II and the same per-candidate evaluation timeout. Unless otherwise stated, LLM-based AOD results are averaged over four independent trials. For inference, initialization states are excluded, and the evolved harness state with the smallest normalized Euclidean distance to the empirical ideal point is selected from the remaining harness states. The selected harness is then applied to both the source task and its corresponding transfer task. For all MOEA solvers, a uniform timeout of 3,600 seconds is imposed on each test run across all test instances. Additional configuration and implementation details are provided in Appendix H.

C. Limitations and Future Directions 

Model accessibility. The Proposer and Realizer currently use proprietary language models, making harness self-evolution dependent on external API access and cost. Adapting smaller or open weight models to these roles, potentially through distillation of successful harness design trajectories, could make the framework more accessible. 

Evaluation cost and cross-task memory transfer. Candidate evaluation remains computationally demanding, as assessing a designed candidate requires executing it on multiple problem instances. While HEMO reuses accumulated memory within a task, our cross-task experiments transfer the harness rather than the task-specific memory accumulated during search. A promising extension is a transferable memory filesystem that distinguishes reusable design knowledge from observations and evaluation records specific to each task. Such a mechanism could reduce redundant exploration and evaluation on new tasks, while requiring safeguards against negative transfer from incompatible prior experience. 

Generalization across tasks. Although HEMO-SEMO out-performs PMOCO in several settings, including the larger size Bi-TSP and Tri-TSP experiments, its advantage is not uniform across problem families. PMOCO remains stronger on Bi-CVRP, indicating that the relative effectiveness of the two approaches depends on the target problem. An important direction is therefore to investigate how design datasets for operators and evaluators can better support generalization across instance sizes and distributions. In particular, more diverse design instances and evaluation criteria that explicitly assess robustness to distribution shifts may help produce more broadly transferable operators. VI. C ONCLUSION 

We present HEMO, a stateful harness self-evolution frame-work for LLM-based automatic operator design in multi-objective combinatorial optimization. By adapting the harness itself through localized state transitions while preserving long-term optimization experience in a memory filesystem, HEMO enables search control to co-evolve with the operator design process. Comparing against state-of-the-art LLM-based AOD methods, HEMO achieves strong performance across diverse tasks and two different evolutionary backbones, while also exhibiting effective intra-family cross-task transfer. We believe HEMO opens a promising direction in which the harness itself becomes a persistent and adaptive optimization object, enabling more effective long-horizon and transferable operator design. APPENDIX CMULTI -O BJECTIVE OPTIMIZATION BACKBONES 

HEMO is evaluated with two multi-objective evolutionary backbones, SEMO and NSGA-II, which expose substantially different search structures and operator design spaces. This section describes their procedures and experimental configurations, and discusses why both backbones are considered. 

A. SEMO 

Simple Evolutionary Multi-objective Optimization (SEMO) is a minimalist Pareto-based evolutionary algorithm that maintains a set of mutually non-dominated solutions [35]. Let Pt denote the Pareto archive at iteration t. Each SEMO iteration consists of the following steps: 1) Parent selection. A solution x ∈ P t is selected from the current archive. 2) Variation. A problem-specific mutation or neighborhood operator is applied to x to produce a candidate solution x′.3) Evaluation. The candidate is checked for feasibility and its multi-objective fitness vector f (x′) is evaluated. 4) Pareto archive update. If x′ is dominated by an existing archive member, it is rejected. Otherwise, x′ is inserted and all solutions dominated by it are removed. The archive update can be expressed compactly as 

Pt+1 = ND ( Pt ∪ { x′}) , (32) where ND( ·) denotes the non-dominated subset. SEMO is particularly suitable for HEMO for several reasons. First, its deliberately simple control structure limits manually engineered population-management mechanisms, making changes induced by the AOD harness easier to isolate. Second, SEMO follows a lightweight incremental update in which only one offspring is generated and evaluated at each iteration. This is especially attractive for harness evaluation, where each generated heuristic must be executed repeatedly across optimization instances. The original analysis of SEMO also shows that, despite its simplicity, it can achieve running time of the same order as a multistart (1 + 1) -EA on the studied bi-objective model problems [35]. Third, SEMO maintains a variable-size archive containing the nondominated solutions discovered so far, without requiring a fixed population size or additional environmental selection. This provides a direct representation of the evolving trade-offs while keeping the underlying solver mechanics minimal. In our implementation, each SEMO run is initialized with 100 randomly generated feasible solutions. Candidate solutions are then generated and evaluated sequentially, with the Pareto archive updated after every iteration. We distinguish the relatively small search budget used to evaluate candidates during harness self-evolution from the substantially larger budget used for final evaluation. B. NSGA-II 

NSGA-II is an elitist, population-based multi-objective evolutionary algorithm [7]. Its design combines non-dominated sorting with diversity preservation through crowding distance. Unlike SEMO, which accepts or rejects every candidate immediately, NSGA-II collects an entire offspring population before performing environmental selection at the generation level. Given a parent population Pt of size N , one generation proceeds as follows: 1) Parent selection. Parent solutions are selected by tournament selection. For the knapsack, routing, and TSP implementations, tournament fitness is determined by non-dominated rank. In the FJSP implementation, rank remains the primary criterion and crowding distance is used to break ties in favor of solutions located in less populated regions of objective space. 2) Variation. Problem-specific crossover and mutation operators are applied to the selected parents to construct an offspring population Qt.3) Population merging. C. Why Two Backbones? 

SEMO and NSGA-II differ in both evolutionary control and the structure of the operator design problem exposed to HEMO. SEMO performs an incremental Pareto archive update after every candidate evaluation, producing a tightly coupled loop between candidate generation and acceptance. NSGA-II instead evaluates variation through a generational population process with non-dominated sorting and diversity preservation, introducing delayed interactions at the population level. The two backbones also represent different levels of algorithmic composition. Under SEMO, the AOD process primarily evolves a single problem-specific neighborhood operator that determines how candidate solutions are generated from the current archive. Under NSGA-II, the solver contains multiple evolvable operators whose effects depend on the surrounding operator configuration. The harness must therefore additionally decide which component to improve and reason about interactions among operator candidates. These differences in solver structure and design granularity also change the information available to the AOD harness. Under SEMO, the harness can reason over a persistent non-dominated archive and the direct consequence of individual neighborhood moves. Under NSGA-II, it must additionally account for interactions at the population level, multiple variation operators, and the delayed effect of environmental selection. Evaluating HEMO under both settings tests whether a stateful AOD harness can adapt effectively across both single-operator and multi-operator design, as well as across substantially different evolutionary control structures. This reduces the risk that the learned harness behavior is specific to the idiosyncrasies of one solver architecture. HEMO assigns credit to the decision module updated at each outer step according to the downstream multi-objective performance of the resulting harness state. The improvement from parent to child is normalized and clipped to [−1, 1] for each score dimension, and the module reward is their average. Hence, rt ∈ [−1, 1] . Key Takeaway. Discounting provides two properties that are useful for stateful harness self-evolution. Credit collected under earlier harness states is attenuated geometrically, and modules that have not been selected recently receive larger exploration bonuses as their discounted counts decrease, making them more likely to be selected again. Together, these effects allow the scheduler to respond when the utility of a module changes after other parts of the harness have been updated. These results do not imply that discounted UCB universally dominates standard UCB. In a stationary setting, retaining the complete history can provide a more accurate estimate of a fixed module utility. In HEMO, however, module utility depends on the evolving harness state, so discounting exchanges some statistical efficiency under stationarity for greater responsiveness to changing module utility. This motivation is consistent with established analyses of discounted UCB for nonstationary bandit problems [57]. APPENDIX HEXPERIMENTAL DETAILS 

A. Memory Filesystem and Record Schema 

HEMO maintains a filesystem-based memory that persists optimization experience across successive harness states. Fixed evaluation API and memory logging routines ensure that different harness states produce records under a common schema. Evaluated candidates are stored as structured candidate records following the MemoryRecord schema, while diagnostic summaries and stagnation reports are maintained as distinct report records within the same memory filesystem. Table XIII summarizes the fields most relevant to experience retrieval and the memory context exposed to subsequent AOD steps. The schema combines task information, evaluation outcomes, search lineage, and multi-objective status. In particular, 

improved_archive , on_pareto_pf , high_hv , and low_runtime provide compact descriptors of a candidate’s contribution and current position in the candidate archive. The Pareto related annotations are updated as the active archive changes, while the original score vector remains available for detailed comparison. These Pareto annotations expose whether a candidate contributes to the current nondominated archive and which region of the quality–efficiency trade-off it represents. Such information is useful because multi-objective archives are designed not only to retain high-quality solutions, but also to preserve convergence and diversity across competing objectives [64], [65]. In HEMO, these archive statistics are retained as explicit memory attributes so that later search decisions can access this information directly. For backbones with multiple interacting operators, the memory additionally records the target operator through target_operator and the operator composition mainly through operator_constitution and 

combination_context . This preserves the context in which an operator produced its observed performance, allowing subsequent search decisions to reason about interactions among algorithmic components instead of treating each operator candidate in isolation. Such context is important because alternative designs of the same operator can exhibit different strengths and inheritance relationships, while operators with different functional roles may interact through complementary, conflicting, or mutually exclusive effects [19]. Prior work on multi-operator evolutionary search similarly shows that combining operators with complementary behaviors and coordinating their usage can be critical to overall search performance [66], [67].

A. Bi-TSP with SEMO: HEMO and MPaGE 

Both selected heuristics first filter the solution archive by Pareto dominance and use randomly weighted, normalized tour costs to select a parent tour. They differ mainly in neighborhood construction, with MPaGE sampling from a broader menu, whereas HEMO pairs block relocation with segment reversal and explicitly removes duplicate neighbors. Neither implementation requires improvement over the parent, as each returns the best sampled neighbor with a swap fallback if it coincides with the parent. Thus, the comparison concerns how a bounded neighborhood is explored.

Compared with MPaGE, HEMO uses a similar sampling budget to explore two complementary changes to each sampled segment, moving it elsewhere in the tour or reversing its order. B. Bi-KP with NSGA-II: HEMO and E2OC 

The selected combinations contain three binary-chromosome operators: kp_bitflip , kp_crossover , and 

kp_repair_mutation . Both designs use profit and weight information. The principal distinction is where randomized profit scalarization enters the combination and whether repair preserves the incoming chromosome. E2OC constructs new greedy solutions in its repair operator, whereas HEMO combines scalarized construction with mutation and uses its final repair operator to modify the incoming solution. 

E2OC / Bi-KP / NSGA-II – Combination Design 

Mutation. Build a greedy core using summed-profit density, insert core items when capacity permits, and prune overweight selections. With probability 0.3, perturb approximately 4% of the bits and repair/refill. 

Crossover. Use min–max-normalized summed-profit density as the per-item probability of copying from the first parent. Generate the two children with separate masks, then prune and greedily refill each child. 

Repair/mutation.
